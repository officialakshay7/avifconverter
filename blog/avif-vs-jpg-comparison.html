AVIF vs JPG: Which Format Should You Actually Use?

Aug 2026 · 6 min read

If you've been exporting images for a website, an app, or just tidying up a photo library, you've probably run into the choice between AVIF and JPG at some point. Both are designed for photographic content, but they were built two decades apart with very different constraints in mind. Here's a practical, no-nonsense comparison to help you decide which one actually fits your situation.

The short answer

AVIF generally produces smaller files at equivalent visual quality, thanks to more advanced compression technology inherited from the AV1 video codec. JPG is older, less efficient by comparison, but supported by essentially every piece of software that has ever touched an image file. If compatibility is your top priority, JPG wins. If file size and modern browser delivery are your priority, AVIF wins. Most people end up needing both at different points, which is why having a fast way to convert between them matters more than picking a permanent favorite.

Compression efficiency

This is where AVIF's advantage is clearest. Independent testing and benchmarks from organizations like Google's own compression research team have found AVIF files can be roughly 40-50% smaller than JPG files while maintaining comparable perceived quality. The gap tends to be largest in images with fine detail, subtle gradients, or large areas of similar color, where JPG's older block-based compression tends to introduce visible artifacts — blockiness, banding, or a slightly "smeared" look — well before AVIF starts to show any degradation at the same file size.

For website owners, this efficiency gap translates directly into faster page loads, since images are typically the single largest contributor to a page's total download size. That's a major reason so many content management systems and CDNs began automatically serving AVIF to browsers that support it.

Compatibility and support

JPG's advantage here is hard to overstate: it has been supported by virtually every operating system, browser, image editor, printer, camera, and messaging app since the mid-1990s. There's essentially no software you'll encounter that can't open a JPG file.

AVIF's browser support is genuinely strong at this point — Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari can all display it natively. But browser support isn't the whole picture. A long tail of other software — certain design tools, print production systems, older versions of common apps, and various upload forms across e-commerce and social platforms — hasn't caught up yet. That's usually the exact moment someone needs to convert an AVIF file back to JPG: not because AVIF is broken, but because something downstream in their workflow expects the older format.

Quality at a given file size

At matched file sizes, AVIF tends to preserve more fine detail and produce fewer visible compression artifacts than JPG, particularly in the shadow and midtone regions of a photo where JPG's block-based approach tends to struggle. This becomes more noticeable at lower quality settings — a heavily compressed JPG will typically show more obvious artifacting than an equally-sized AVIF file.

At high quality settings, both formats can look excellent, and the practical difference becomes harder to spot without pixel-level comparison. If you're already using a high JPG quality setting and file size isn't a pressing concern, the visual difference between the two formats may not be worth agonizing over.

Color and dynamic range support

AVIF supports high dynamic range (HDR) content and wider color gamuts than JPG can represent, which matters increasingly as more displays capable of showing that extra range become common in phones, laptops, and TVs. JPG is limited to a narrower, older color specification that was more than adequate for the displays of the 1990s but can't take full advantage of what modern screens are capable of.

For most everyday photography and web use, this difference won't be something you notice directly, but it's part of why AVIF is considered a more future-proof format from a technical standpoint.

Editing and software support

JPG remains the safer choice if you need broad editing support. Nearly every image editor, from basic built-in OS tools to professional software, opens and saves JPG without issue. AVIF editing support has improved but is still less universal — some tools can open AVIF files but not save them, or require a plugin or recent update to handle the format at all.

When to choose JPG

  • You're uploading to a platform, marketplace, or form that doesn't clearly support AVIF.
  • You're sending the image to someone using older or unspecified software.
  • You need it to open reliably in a print shop's intake system.
  • You're working with an email client or app known to render AVIF poorly.

When to choose AVIF

  • You're optimizing images for a modern website where page speed matters.
  • You want to save on storage space for a large photo library.
  • You're delivering HDR or wide-gamut content to compatible displays.
  • You control the full pipeline and know AVIF will be supported end to end.

The practical middle ground

In reality, most people don't need to pick a single format forever — they need the right one for a specific situation. A photographer might store their working library as AVIF to save space, but need to export a JPG for a client who's uploading to a platform that hasn't added AVIF support. A web developer might serve AVIF to visitors for speed while keeping a JPG fallback for older browsers. Both formats have a legitimate, ongoing role.
